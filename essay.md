In the spring of 1941, the United States federal government utilized eminent domain to condemn roughly 100,000 acres across Onslow County, North Carolina, to establish Marine Barracks New River – which would later become Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune. With approximately 500 families [^1] receiving condemnation notices and compensatory payments (values as deemed fit by the government), land full of agrarian, maritime, and multi-generational family history was abruptly turned into a restricted national-defense zone. The contemporary map of Camp Lejeune is not simply a military footprint, but rather a boundary of civilian dispossession. By observing the erasure of domestic spaces into tactical zones, the migration of displaced families to perimeter corridors, and the severed maritime waterways, it becomes evident that the construction of Camp Lejeune permanently reorganized the social, racial, and economic landscape of coastal North Carolina.
Prior to 1941, the southern basin of New River consisted of family farms, schools, churches, and fishing settlements. The civilian village of Marines, North Carolina, anchored fishing and farming families across the New River, while new outposts grew at Courthouse Bay, Duck Creek, and Hadnot Point. When the U.S. government condemned the basin, the landscape was overwritten almost overnight. Courthouse Bay and Hadnot Point were repurposed as tactical operations and headquarters facilities, while Marines ceased to exist on state maps. It was an enforced militarization of space.

Camp Lejeune’s internal geography also reflects how wartime mobilization operated under rigid racial divisions. While white Marine units were integrated across the central quarters of Hadnot Point, Black recruits entering the newly desegregated Marine Corps under Executive Order 8802 were stationed on the base’s northeastern periphery at Montford Point (later renamed Camp Gilbert H. Johnson). Montford Point’s physical detachment from the main operational core demonstrates how defense infrastructure reinforced Jim Crow segregation through spatial design. Montford Point sat isolated in dense woodland and swamp, requiring separate access roads, distinct facilities, and isolated administration. The geography of the installation also shows how the federal transformation of Onslow County didn’t merely disrupt civilian life, but it reinforced national patterns of segregation directly into the physical layout of the base.

Tracing the map outwards reveals where the dispossessed population landed. When the hundreds of families were forced out in 1941, many simply settled along the base’s northern and western borders. Displaced populations were concentrated along major access roads and rail lines, rapidly expanding edge communities like Verona, Kellumtown, Sneads Ferry, and Jacksonville. Within the resettled population, racial and economic patterns are recurring. Displaced white landowners with greater financial capital would buy farmland along transit corridors closer to Verona, or move into Jacksonville where they might participate in the wartime service academy. Black landowners and displaced tenant farmers were disproportionately channeled into rural communities such as Kellumtown near Hubert.

[^1]: Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune, “Before There Was Camp Lejeune, There Were Landowners.”

## What my map cannot show

While the map captures official property boundaries and established points, it cannot adequately convey the informal, unrecorded movements of displaced families – particularly tenant farmers and sharecroppers who held no deeds and frequently vanished from county court compensation records. Because many early evictions were carried out rapidly with 30-day notices and nominal checks, exact migration paths for poorer Black families to areas like Kellumtown are reconstructed through later oral histories and census records rather than formal boundary surveys. Additionally, the points do not show the gradual economic erosion experienced by local oystermen and fishermen who relied on unrestricted access to the New River before perimeter water patrols began. The map provides the clear geometry of the base, but it cannot show the informal civilian networks and everyday spaces that were dismantled before military cartographers redrew the area.
