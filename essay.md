In the spring of 1941, the United States federal government utilized eminent domain to condemn roughly 100,000 acres across Onslow County, North Carolina, to establish Marine Barracks New River – which would later become Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune. With approximately 500 families [^1] receiving condemnation notices and compensatory payments (values as deemed fit by the government), land full of agrarian, maritime, and multi-generational family history was abruptly turned into a restricted national-defense zone. The contemporary map of Camp Lejeune is not simply a military footprint, but rather a boundary of civilian dispossession. By observing the erasure of domestic spaces into tactical zones, the migration of displaced families to perimeter corridors, and the severed maritime waterways, it becomes evident that the construction of Camp Lejeune permanently reorganized the social, racial, and economic landscape of coastal North Carolina.

[^1]: Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune, “Before There Was Camp Lejeune, There Were Landowners.”
